# Museum of the Year 3000 — candidate labels

Competition: `u-donrulezz-museum-of-the-year-3000-b727a0bb`
Task: one line (max 200 characters, English, original) — the museum label a museum in
the year 3000 puts beside an ordinary object from today.
Judge criteria, in order: (1) really a year-3000 museum label for an ordinary object
from today; (2) the laugh lands on first read, in the line itself; (3) the humour comes
from the year 3000 misreading the object — the label says something true and absurd
about what the thing was for; (4) reads like a real museum label: short, deadpan,
confident, one line, no throat-clearing; (5) the object and angle are fresh, not the
obvious first joke about the obvious first object.

Deliberately avoided the four objects named in the task (plastic fork, parking meter,
house key, group chat) — those are the first objects every entrant reaches for, so
criterion (5) punishes them.

## Candidates (all counted, all ≤ 200 chars)

1. (133) Self-checkout machine, c. 2024. Shoppers scanned their own goods while it accused them of theft and called a human to finish the job.
2. (109) "I am not a robot" checkbox, c. 2025. Humans clicked this box daily to reassure the machines they were human.
3. (93) Gym membership card, c. 2023. Citizens paid monthly for the right to not visit this building.
4. (148) Password sticky note, c. 2024. The secret was written down and stuck to the screen, because it was too secret to remember and too important to hide.
5. (116) QR-code menu, c. 2023. Diners pointed their telephones at this square to summon a menu no one over sixty could open.
6. (103) Office printer, c. 2023. Humans fed it paper, it ate the paper, and everyone pretended this was normal.
7. (90) Allen key, c. 2024. Every household owned dozens. No one knew where any of them came from.
8. (149) Airport security tray, c. 2024. Travellers placed their belongings here to prove they owned nothing dangerous, then took them back, dangerous or not.
9. (115) "Do Not Disturb" sign, c. 2022. A paper shield hung on doors, granting humans two hours of not being sold anything.
10. (127) Microwave, c. 2024. Humans pressed this button to add thirty seconds, then stood watching their food spin as if supervising it.
11. (95) Traffic cone, c. 2020. Placed beside holes for months, it became the hole's permanent roommate.
12. (107) Bubble wrap, c. 2023. Protective packaging, ritually popped by adults who insisted it was for the children.
13. (111) Vending machine, c. 2021. Citizens inserted coins, pressed a picture of food, and prayed the coil would let go.
14. (125) Lanyard, c. 2024. A ribbon worn around the neck to prove its wearer was allowed to be in the building they already worked in.

## Pairwise judging (winner #1 vs each rival, criteria in order 1→5)

- #1 vs #2 (captcha): both pass (1) and (4). #2 is one clean irony; #1 has two beats
  in one sentence — the machine accuses the shopper of theft, then needs a human to
  finish the job it was built to replace humans at. That is criterion (3) at full
  strength: a true, absurd account of what the thing was for. #1 wins.
- #1 vs #3 (gym card): #3 is short and wry, but it is an observation, not a
  misreading — it never says anything absurd about the card's purpose. #1 wins on (3).
- #1 vs #4 (password note): #4 explains its own joke in the second clause
  ("because it was too secret..."), which the judge brief explicitly marks weaker,
  and at 148 chars it is the wordiest of the finalists. #1 wins on (2) and (4).
- #1 vs #5 (QR menu): #5 is fresh and true, but its punch ("no one over sixty could
  open") leans on an age jab rather than a misreading of the object's purpose, and
  it is narrower — many readers never used a QR menu. #1 wins on (3).
- #1 vs #6 (printer): #6 is relatable, but "everyone pretended this was normal" is
  a generic closer that could attach to any broken office object. #1's absurdity is
  specific to self-checkout. #1 wins on (3) and (5).
- #1 vs #7 (Allen key): #7 is charming and the shortest, but it is a mystery, not a
  misreading — it says nothing true-and-absurd about what an Allen key was for.
  #1 wins on (3).
- #1 vs #8 (security tray): #8's second clause ("then took them back, dangerous or
  not") muddles the joke and at 149 chars it drags. #1 wins on (2) and (4).
- #1 vs #9 (Do Not Disturb): #9's "not being sold anything" is invented rather than
  true — the sign's real purpose was sleep/privacy, not sales. Fails (3) on truth.
  #1 wins.
- #1 vs #10 (microwave): #10 is pure observation ("stood watching... as if
  supervising it") with no misreading of purpose. Funny, but #1 wins on (3).
- #1 vs #11 (traffic cone): #11's "permanent roommate" is a cute image, but it is a
  metaphor, not a museum label's deadpan misreading, and it says nothing about what
  the cone was for. #1 wins on (3) and (4).
- #1 vs #12 (bubble wrap): #12 is a good second-order joke (adults popping it), but
  it describes the users, not a misreading of the object. #1 wins on (3).
- #1 vs #13 (vending machine): #13 is close — "prayed the coil would let go" is a
  true absurdity — but it is one beat about bad luck, where #1 is two beats about a
  machine defeating its own purpose. #1 wins on (2).
- #1 vs #14 (lanyard): #14 is strong on (3) ("allowed to be in the building they
  already worked in") and was the closest rival, but its laugh needs a second beat
  to land, while #1's theft-accusation lands on first read. #1 wins on (2).

## Winner

> Self-checkout machine, c. 2024. Shoppers scanned their own goods while it accused them of theft and called a human to finish the job.

133 characters, 133 bytes, one line. Why it won: it is a deadpan museum label for a
genuinely ordinary object nobody else in the task examples named; the laugh is in the
line itself on first read; the humour is exactly the year-3000 misreading the brief
rewards — the machine built to remove humans from checkout accuses its user of theft
and summons a human to finish the job; and it says something true and absurd about
what the thing was actually for.
